The findings

Eight patterns we see in owner-led companies, each written the same way: what we see, why it persists, what we'd do first, and how we'd know it worked.

01

Every decision still goes through the founder

What we see
Approvals queue at one desk. Teams wait, or work around the queue and hope.
Why it persists
It worked when the company was small, and nobody has written down what the founder actually checks for.
What we would do first
Write down the founder's real criteria for the ten most common decisions, then hand three of them down.
How we would know it worked
Those decisions get made a level down and are not quietly re-checked afterwards.

02

The numbers arrive too late to act on

What we see
Month-end reports land weeks after the month, and are argued about rather than used.
Why it persists
Reporting grew one spreadsheet at a time, each owned by someone who has since moved on.
What we would do first
Agree the handful of weekly signals that would have changed a recent decision, and get those first.
How we would know it worked
Leaders refer to the weekly view in meetings without being reminded it exists.

03

Growth has outrun the process

What we see
The way work is handed from sales to delivery was designed for a much smaller team.
Why it persists
Everyone is too busy delivering to stop and redesign the thing that makes them busy.
What we would do first
Map the handoff with both sides in the room and remove the steps nobody can explain.
How we would know it worked
Fewer jobs bounce back for missing information, and the teams stop blaming each other for it.

04

Nobody owns the handoffs between teams

What we see
Each team runs its own part well. The gaps between them belong to no one.
Why it persists
Roles were drawn around people, not around the work that passes between them.
What we would do first
Name an owner for each handoff and give them the authority to change it.
How we would know it worked
Problems at the boundary get raised by the owner, not discovered by the customer.

05

The plan lives in one person's head

What we see
The owners know where the company is going. The managers are guessing.
Why it persists
Writing it down feels like a constraint, and the last attempt became a document no one read.
What we would do first
Put the plan on a few pages, in plain words, with the decisions it implies.
How we would know it worked
Managers make trade-offs that match the plan without asking which way to go.

06

Pricing hasn't been revisited in years

What we see
Prices were set at launch and nudged since. Nobody is sure what the work now costs to deliver.
Why it persists
Changing prices feels risky, and there is never a quiet month to look at it properly.
What we would do first
Work out what each type of job really takes to deliver, using the records you already keep.
How we would know it worked
Pricing conversations start from the cost of delivery rather than from last year's quote.

07

Busy work hides the real bottleneck

What we see
Everyone is flat out, yet work still waits. The constraint is somewhere nobody is looking.
Why it persists
Being busy looks like being productive, so the queue in front of one step goes unnoticed.
What we would do first
Follow a handful of jobs end to end and find where they sit still the longest.
How we would know it worked
The team can name the bottleneck, and the work in front of it stops piling up.

08

New hires take too long to find their feet

What we see
New starters shadow whoever has time and learn a different version of the job from each.
Why it persists
How the work is done lives in people's habits, not anywhere a newcomer can read it.
What we would do first
Write down the first month of the role with the people who do it best.
How we would know it worked
New starters work on their own sooner, and ask the same questions less often.
